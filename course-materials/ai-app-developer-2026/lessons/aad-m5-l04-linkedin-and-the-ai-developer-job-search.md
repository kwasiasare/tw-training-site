## From Portfolio to Pipeline

The last three lessons built the proof: a production capstone, a packaged portfolio, and a resume written around impact. This lesson builds the **distribution**. Most strong candidates lose not on capability but on **visibility** — their work is real, but no recruiter ever sees it, or the profile recruiters do see says nothing. Your job now is to make your LinkedIn profile a thirty-second argument that you can do the role, then run a deliberate search that puts that argument in front of the right people. Treat it like engineering: a clean interface (the profile), a search strategy, and a repeatable loop.

## Rewrite Your Profile as an AI Developer

**Headline.** The most-searched, most-skimmed field on LinkedIn. Name the role you want, your stack, and your edge. For example: *AI Application Developer | LLM apps, RAG & agents | Python, FastAPI, OpenAI + Claude, vector DBs*. It is keyword-dense so you surface in recruiter searches, and legible so a human keeps reading. Do not leave it as an unrelated current job title.

**About.** Open with one positioning line, then prove it with your projects in specific, quantified language, then name your stack and any credential trajectory (e.g., AI-102 in progress), then your target titles, and end by pointing at the evidence. The aim is that a reader finishes the first two sentences already believing you do this work. (The short-answer question in this lesson is exactly this exercise — write yours.)

**Featured.** This is your portfolio strip and the single highest-leverage section for a career-changer. Add your projects as Featured items: each GitHub repo and the short demo video, plus a live demo link. A reviewer who clicks Featured and sees a clean repo, an architecture diagram, and a 90-second demo is most of the way to inviting you to a screen.

**Experience and Projects.** Use LinkedIn's Projects section (and Experience where appropriate) to list each app with the same structure as your resume: a one-line problem, the technology, and a quantified result — *'Built a RAG assistant (FastAPI, pgvector, OpenAI) scoring 0.9 faithfulness on a golden set, deployed to a public URL.'* Self-directed projects are legitimate experience; present them as such.

**Skills.** Add the skills that match real job descriptions: Python, LLMs, RAG, prompt engineering, OpenAI API, Anthropic/Claude, vector databases, FastAPI, LangChain/LangGraph, Docker, evaluation. Pin the most important ones, because skills drive recruiter search and the 'how you match' panel on applications.

**Open to Work.** Turn on Open to Work (recruiters-only visibility if you are currently employed), set your target titles and locations, and you immediately enter the dedicated recruiter pool.

## Target the Right Titles

The same skill set is posted under several titles. Search and set alerts for **AI Engineer**, **AI Application Developer**, **LLM Engineer**, **Software Engineer - AI / ML**, **Applied AI Engineer**, **GenAI Engineer**, and sometimes **Machine Learning Engineer (LLM)**. Read ten real postings for your target title and note the recurring tools and phrases — that vocabulary is what to mirror (truthfully) in your profile and resume. If most postings say 'RAG' and 'function calling,' those exact terms belong in your profile because you have done them.

## Mirror the Job Description (Honestly)

Both LinkedIn Recruiter searches and ATS rank you on keyword relevance. For each application, align your profile and resume language to the posting's real terms — **only** for skills you genuinely have and can defend. This is alignment, not invention. If a JD emphasizes 'evaluation' or 'MCP' and you implemented those, use those phrases. If it lists a tool you have never touched, that is a gap to learn, not a word to fake.

## Outreach That Gets Replies

Applications alone are a low-yield channel; warm outreach multiplies your odds. Keep messages short, specific, and respectful:

- **To a recruiter on a role:** *'Hi NAME, I saw the AI Engineer opening on TEAM. I've shipped a RAG assistant, an autonomous research agent, and a deployed production AI app with an eval suite (links in my profile). Would you be open to a quick chat about fit?'*
- **To an engineer on the team:** reference something real — a talk they gave, a tool the team uses, a blog post — then a one-line fit statement and a small ask (*'any advice on what your team values in an AI hire?'*).

Never send identical boilerplate to hundreds of people; it reads as spam and burns your name. Ten specific messages beat two hundred generic ones.

## Prepare for the Screens

**Recruiter screen.** A fit-and-communication filter: why this role, your salary range, location/remote, notice period, and whether you can explain your work clearly to a non-expert. Have a crisp two-minute story and a salary range ready.

**Technical screen.** Where depth is tested. Expect to **walk through your architecture** — your RAG pipeline or agent loop — and justify decisions with the alternatives you rejected (the same intent → design → trade-offs reasoning from your design docs). Expect, depending on the company: a **live coding** exercise (often Python — parse and call an API, write a small function, handle errors), and/or a **system-design discussion** such as *'design a RAG system for customer support'* where you talk through ingestion, chunking, embeddings, retrieval, the model call, evaluation, and cost. Be ready to discuss **evaluation and hallucination** specifically — how you measure faithfulness and reduce ungrounded answers — because it is the question that separates people who have shipped from people who have only demoed.

## Build in Public to Get Found

The highest-leverage thing you can do beyond applying is to **be visible**. Recruiters increasingly find AI developers through their public work, not just their applications. Post short, specific notes about what you are building: a chunking change that lifted retrieval precision, an eval result, a gotcha you hit wiring up function calling, a small MCP server you wrote. You do not need a large following — one useful post that an engineer reshares can put you in front of a hiring team you would never have reached by applying. Comment thoughtfully on posts from people at companies you admire; a genuine, technical comment is a softer, more effective introduction than a cold message. Over a few weeks this 'build in public' habit compounds: your profile fills with evidence, your name starts appearing in the right feeds, and inbound messages from recruiters begin to replace the cold outbound grind. It is the same portfolio work you already did, simply made visible.

## Run a Weekly Loop

Momentum comes from a routine, not a sprint. A workable weekly loop:

- **Apply** to 5-10 well-matched roles with tailored language.
- **Send** 5-10 specific outreach messages.
- **Post or comment once** on an AI-developer topic (a short note on a detection you tuned, an eval result, a RAG lesson learned) to stay visible and signal expertise.
- **Extend one project slightly** so your Featured section keeps improving.
- **Track** every application in a simple sheet (role, company, date, contact, status) so you follow up deliberately.

The search is a pipeline; keep it full and keep moving candidates through it.

## The Deliverable

By the end of this lesson you should have a rewritten profile (headline, About, Featured with your projects linked, Projects/Experience entries, skills, Open to Work on), a shortlist of target titles with saved searches, a tailored keyword pass for your top postings, a small set of specific outreach messages sent, and a weekly routine on the calendar. That is the machine that turns the portfolio you built into interviews — and the interviews into your first AI developer offer.
