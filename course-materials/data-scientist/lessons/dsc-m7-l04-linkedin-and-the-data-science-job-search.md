## From Portfolio to Pipeline

Your capstone, your portfolio, and your resume prove capability. This lesson builds **distribution**: the system that puts that proof in front of the right people and converts it into interviews. Most strong candidates lose not on skill but on visibility, their work is real but no recruiter ever sees it, or the profile recruiters do see says nothing. Treat the job search like engineering: a clear interface (your LinkedIn profile), a targeting strategy (the right titles and keywords), and a repeatable weekly loop.

## Rewrite Your Profile as a Data Scientist

**Headline.** This is the most-searched, most-skimmed field on LinkedIn. Name the role you want, your edge, and your key tools: *Data Scientist | Python, SQL, ML & Experimentation | Churn, Forecasting, RAG*. It is keyword-dense so you appear in recruiter searches and legible so a human keeps reading. Do not leave it as an unrelated current job title.

**About.** Open with one positioning line, then prove it with two or three quantified projects spanning classical ML, experimentation, and GenAI, then name your target role and point at the evidence. Aim for a reader to finish the first two sentences already believing you do this work. (The short-answer exercise in this lesson is exactly this, write yours.)

**Featured.** This is your portfolio strip and the highest-leverage section for a career-changer. Add your projects as Featured links, each GitHub repo and, ideally, a live Streamlit demo. A reviewer who clicks Featured and finds a clean repo, an architecture or workflow diagram, and a working demo is most of the way to inviting you to a screen.

**Experience and Projects.** Use the Projects section (and Experience where appropriate) to list each project with the same structure as your resume: a one-line problem, the technology, and a quantified result. Self-directed projects are legitimate experience, present them as such.

**Skills and Open to Work.** Add the skills that match real DS postings, Python, SQL, machine learning, statistics, A/B testing, pandas, scikit-learn, data visualization, MLflow, and pin the most important. Turn on **Open to Work** (recruiters-only if you are currently employed), set target titles and locations, and you immediately appear in the dedicated recruiter pool.

## Link Your GitHub and Kaggle

A data scientist's profiles are proof, not decoration. Link your **GitHub** prominently and make sure your pinned repos are your best, reproducible projects. If you have a **Kaggle** presence, a clean public notebook or a competition entry, link it too; it is the most recognized public proving ground in the field and signals you can communicate analysis to a broad audience. These links let a recruiter verify your claims in seconds, which is exactly what moves a profile view toward a screen.

## Target the Right Titles

The same skill set is posted under several titles. Search and set alerts for **Data Scientist**, **Applied Scientist**, **Machine Learning Engineer**, **Data Analyst** and **Product Data Analyst**, **Decision Scientist**, and **Analytics / Insights** roles. Early in a career, analyst and junior data scientist titles are often the most accessible entry points and a strong springboard. Read ten real postings for your target title and note the recurring tools and phrases, that vocabulary is what you mirror, truthfully, in your profile and resume.

## Mirror the Job Description (Honestly)

Both LinkedIn Recruiter searches and applicant tracking systems rank you on keyword relevance. For each application, align your profile and resume language to the posting's real terms, *only* for skills you genuinely have and can defend. If a JD emphasizes *experimentation* and *causal inference* and you ran an A/B test in your capstone, use those phrases. If it lists a tool you have never touched, that is a gap to learn, not a word to fake, because every term must survive the technical screen.

## Outreach That Gets Replies

Applications alone are a low-yield channel; warm outreach multiplies your odds. Keep messages short, specific, and respectful:

- **To a recruiter on a role:** "Hi NAME, I saw the Data Scientist opening on TEAM. I have built a churn model with a live demo, an A/B test analysis, and a small RAG app (links in my profile). Would you be open to a quick chat about fit?"
- **To a data scientist on the team:** reference something real, a talk they gave, a tool the team uses, then a one-line fit statement and a small ask ("any advice on what your team looks for in a data hire?").

Never send identical boilerplate to hundreds of people; it reads as spam. Ten specific messages beat two hundred generic ones.

## Prepare for the Data Science Interview Loop

DS loops are broad. Prepare each format deliberately:

- **Statistics & probability.** Hypothesis testing, p-values and confidence intervals, the bias-variance trade-off, Bayes' rule, common distributions, and how to reason about an experiment. Be ready to explain, not just recite.
- **SQL.** Joins, aggregations, window functions, and writing a correct query under time pressure. Practice on a platform with real prompts; this round screens out a surprising number of candidates.
- **ML case study / product sense.** Given an open business problem ("how would you reduce churn?"), frame it: clarify the goal and metric, propose features and a model, discuss validation, and name the trade-offs. This mirrors the intent-approach-result-trade-offs arc you used for your capstone.
- **Take-home.** A scoped dataset and a question. Treat it like a mini-capstone: clean structure, a clear README, honest evaluation, and a short write-up that leads with the result. Respect the time box.
- **Behavioral.** Use concrete stories (the STAR shape, situation, task, action, result) drawn straight from your projects, and be ready to discuss a failure and what you learned.

The most efficient preparation is rehearsing your own project stories until you can narrate any of them in two minutes, then drilling SQL and stats fundamentals on practice platforms.

## Run a Weekly Loop

Momentum comes from a routine, not a sprint. A workable weekly loop: apply to five to ten well-matched roles with tailored language; send five to ten specific outreach messages; do focused interview practice (a SQL set, a stats topic, one case study); publish or comment once on a data topic to stay visible; and extend one project slightly so your Featured section keeps improving. Track everything in a simple sheet, role, company, date, contact, status, so you follow up deliberately. The search is a pipeline, keep it full and keep moving candidates through it.

## Referrals Beat Cold Applications

One more multiplier deserves its own habit: a referral typically outperforms a cold application by a wide margin, so spend part of every week building genuine connections rather than only clicking apply. Comment thoughtfully on practitioners' posts, share a short write-up of a project you shipped, and join a virtual meetup or community now and then. When you have a warm contact at a target company, a polite request for a referral, attached to your specific, relevant projects, often jumps you past the resume pile entirely. Networking is not a favor you extract; it is a relationship you build before you need it.

## The Deliverable

By the end of this lesson you should have a rewritten LinkedIn profile (headline, About, Featured with projects linked, Projects/Experience entries, skills, Open to Work on, GitHub and Kaggle linked), a shortlist of target titles with saved searches, a keyword pass aligned to your top postings, a small set of specific outreach messages sent, an interview-prep plan across the five formats, and a weekly routine on the calendar. That is the machine that turns the portfolio you built into the offer you accept.
