## The fix is only half the job

A technically perfect resolution delivered rudely is a *bad* support experience. Users remember how you made them feel as much as whether the problem went away. **Communication** and **customer service** are not soft extras on a helpdesk — they are the core of the job. This lesson gives you concrete techniques you can use on your very next call.

## Open and close professionally

Every interaction has a shape. Open warmly, identify yourself, and confirm who you are helping. Close by confirming resolution and inviting follow-up.

```text
Opening:
"Good morning, IT Service Desk, this is Daniel. How can I help today?"

Closing:
"Great, your email is working now. I'll keep the ticket open
until end of day in case anything recurs. Anything else I can help with?"
```

A clear open and close makes you sound calm and in control even when the middle is messy.

## Listen actively and confirm understanding

**Active listening** means giving the user your full attention, letting them finish, and then **restating** the issue in your own words. This catches misunderstandings before you waste time solving the wrong thing.

```text
"So just to confirm - your laptop connects to Wi-Fi at home,
but not in the office since this morning. Is that right?"
```

That one sentence reassures the user you were listening and locks in a shared understanding.

## Drop the jargon

Most users are not technical. Translate. Instead of "your DNS resolver is misconfigured," say "your computer is looking up website addresses in the wrong place — I'll point it to the right one." Plain language respects the user and keeps them with you. Never make someone feel stupid for not knowing IT — that is *your* job, not theirs.

## Set expectations and keep people informed

Uncertainty is what frustrates users most. Always tell them **what happens next and roughly when**. Under-promise and over-deliver.

```text
"This one needs our network team. I'm escalating it now with
all your details. You should hear back within two hours, and
I've put my notes on the ticket so you won't repeat yourself."
```

If you say you will call back, call back — even if it is only to say there is no news yet.

## De-escalating a frustrated user

Angry users are usually frustrated by the *situation*, not by you. Do not take it personally, and never argue or blame other teams. Lead with **empathy**, then move to **action**.

```text
User: "This is the third time this week! It's unacceptable."
You:  "You're right, and I'm sorry - that's genuinely disruptive.
       Let's get you working now, and I'll flag the repeat issue
       so it gets properly investigated."
```

Acknowledge the feeling, take ownership of the next step, and stay calm. Tone of voice matters as much as words.

## Your task

Write a short script for this call: a user phones, clearly stressed, because their presentation file won't open thirty minutes before a big client meeting. Write your opening line, one empathy statement, one sentence confirming the issue back to them, and a closing line that sets expectations. Keep every line jargon-free.
