## From Proof to Pipeline

You now have a documented lab, a portfolio link, and a tailored resume, real proof you can do entry-level IT support. The last gap is visibility: most strong career-changers lose not on capability but because no recruiter ever sees them, or the profile recruiters do see says nothing. This lesson turns your LinkedIn profile into a thirty-second argument that you can do the job, runs a deliberate search that puts that argument in front of the right people, and prepares you for the interview that follows. Treat it as engineering: a clear interface (the profile), a search strategy, and a repeatable loop.

## Rewrite Your Profile as an IT Support Professional

**Headline.** This is the most-searched, most-skimmed field on LinkedIn. Name the role you want, your key tools, and your certifications, for example: *Help Desk / Desktop Support Technician | Windows 11, Active Directory, Microsoft 365 & Intune | CompTIA A+, Network+*. It is keyword-dense so you surface in recruiter searches, and legible so a human keeps reading. Do not leave it as an unrelated current job title.

**About.** Open with one positioning line (who you are and what you support), then prove it with your capstone and projects in specific language, then name your certifications and target role, and end by pointing at your portfolio. Aim for a reader to finish the first two sentences already believing you do this work. Keep it warm and plain, customer service is half of this job, so let your communication show.

**Featured.** This is your portfolio strip and the single highest-leverage section for a career-changer. Add your portfolio repo or site and, ideally, a short demo video. A recruiter who clicks Featured and sees clean runbooks, screenshots, and a three-minute demo is most of the way to inviting you to a screen.

**Experience and Projects.** Use the Projects section (and Experience where appropriate) to list your capstone and labs with the same structure as your resume: a one-line problem, the tools, and a result, each linking to the portfolio. Self-directed projects are legitimate experience; present them as such.

**Skills and Open to Work.** Add the skills that match real postings, Active Directory, Microsoft Intune, Microsoft 365, Windows 11, ticketing systems, ITIL, troubleshooting, PowerShell, and pin the most important. Then turn on **Open to Work** (recruiters-only visibility if you are currently employed), set your target titles and locations, and you immediately appear in the dedicated recruiter pool.

## Target the Right Titles

The same entry-level skill set is posted under several titles. Search and set alerts for **Help Desk Technician**, **Desktop Support Technician / Engineer**, **IT Support Specialist**, **Service Desk Analyst**, and related terms like **Technical Support** and **End User Support**. Read ten real postings for these titles and note the recurring tools and phrases, that vocabulary is what to mirror (truthfully) in your profile and resume. If most postings say 'Active Directory', 'Intune', and 'ticketing', those exact terms belong on your profile because you have done them.

## Mirror the Job Description (Honestly)

Both LinkedIn recruiter searches and applicant tracking systems rank you on keyword relevance. For each application, align your profile and resume language to the posting's real terms, only for skills you genuinely have and can defend. If a JD emphasizes 'Microsoft Intune' and you configured it in your capstone, use that phrase. If it lists a tool you have never touched, that is a gap to learn in your lab, not a word to fake. This is alignment, not invention.

## Outreach That Gets Replies

Applications alone are a low-yield channel; warm outreach multiplies your odds. Keep messages short, specific, and respectful:

- **To a recruiter on a role:** 'Hi NAME, I saw the Help Desk Technician opening at COMPANY. I have hands-on labs in Active Directory, Microsoft 365, and Intune (portfolio linked on my profile) and I hold CompTIA A+ and Network+. Would you be open to a quick chat about fit?'
- **To IT staff at a target company:** reference something real, a tool the team uses or a post they shared, then a one-line fit statement and a small ask ('any advice on what your service desk looks for in a new technician?').

Never send identical boilerplate to hundreds of people; it reads as spam. Ten specific messages beat two hundred generic ones.

## Prepare for the Interview

Entry-level IT support interviews test three things: how you troubleshoot, how you treat people, and whether you know basic service-management vocabulary.

- **Troubleshooting scenarios.** Expect 'A user can't print', 'Outlook won't connect', 'the shared drive is inaccessible'. Answer with your **methodology**: gather information, reproduce, isolate the layer (user, device, network, permissions, service), test a theory, fix, verify, and document. They care how you think more than the one right answer. Use your capstone tickets as concrete examples.
- **Customer service.** Expect 'Tell me about a difficult user' or 'a time you explained something technical to a non-technical person'. Answer with **STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result), and emphasize calm, empathy, and clear communication. Support is a people job as much as a technical one.
- **ITIL basics.** Know the vocabulary from Module 4: the difference between an **incident** (restore service) and a **service request** (a standard ask), what a **problem** is (the underlying cause of repeated incidents), and why **SLAs**, **priority**, and **escalation** exist. You do not need deep ITIL expertise; you need to speak the language confidently.

Prepare a crisp two-minute story of your journey (career-changer to documented, certified, job-ready), have a salary range ready, and prepare two or three questions to ask them. Practice answers aloud, the goal is to sound like someone who has already made these decisions, because in your lab you have.

## Run a Weekly Loop

Momentum comes from a routine, not a sprint. A workable weekly loop: apply to five to ten well-matched roles with tailored language; send five to ten specific outreach messages; extend or polish one portfolio item so your Featured section keeps improving; review or rehearse one set of interview answers (a scenario, a customer-service story, an ITIL term); and track every application in a simple sheet (role, company, date, contact, status) so you follow up deliberately. Stay visible by commenting on or sharing IT topics occasionally. The search is a pipeline, keep it full and keep moving candidates through it.

## Stay Visible and Keep Learning

While you search, keep growing in public. Work a free Microsoft Learn module or a CompTIA objective each week and share one thing you learned; a short post on a fix you practiced keeps you visible and signals genuine enthusiasm to recruiters who check your activity. Engagement compounds, the more useful you are in the feed, the more your profile gets viewed, and views are the top of your interview funnel.

## The Deliverable

By the end of this lesson you should have a rewritten LinkedIn profile (headline, About, Featured with your portfolio linked, Projects entries, skills, Open to Work on), a shortlist of target titles with saved searches, a small set of specific outreach messages sent, a one-page interview-prep sheet (a troubleshooting scenario, a STAR story, the ITIL terms), and a weekly routine on your calendar. That is the machine that turns six months of study and a strong portfolio into your first IT support job.
