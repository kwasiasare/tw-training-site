## The Resume Has Two Readers

Your resume is read twice: first by software, then by a human. The software, an **applicant tracking system (ATS)**, parses your file and often ranks it by how well it matches the job description. The human, a recruiter or hiring manager, skims the survivors for about ten seconds before deciding to read on. To win, your resume must parse cleanly, surface the right keywords, and prove impact fast. This lesson builds exactly that, anchored by the lab and portfolio you already created.

## Quantify Everything You Can

The difference between an ignored resume and an interviewed one is usually **numbers**. 'Helped users with issues' is invisible. 'Resolved 45+ tickets per week at 94% first-contact resolution' is hireable. Support work is rich with metrics, use them:

- **Ticket volume** - tickets handled per day or week (throughput and capacity).
- **First-contact resolution (FCR)** - percentage fixed on first contact (you solve, not bounce).
- **CSAT** - customer satisfaction score, often out of 5 or as a percentage (service quality).
- **SLA attainment** - percentage of tickets resolved within the service-level target (reliability).
- **Average handle / resolution time** - how quickly you close issues (efficiency).
- **Scope numbers** - users or devices supported, mailboxes migrated, endpoints enrolled.

No paid job yet? Use your **capstone** truthfully. You can write: *Built and documented a Windows + Active Directory + Intune lab and resolved 10 realistic support scenarios end to end, each documented as a runbook with verification.* That is real, specific, and quantified. The bullet formula is the same one from earlier in the course: **action verb + what you did + the technology + the measurable result.**

Examples tuned for helpdesk and desktop support:

- 'Resolved 45+ Tier 1/2 tickets weekly across Windows 11, Microsoft 365, and Intune, sustaining 94% first-contact resolution and 4.7/5 CSAT.'
- 'Onboarded and offboarded users in Active Directory and Microsoft 365, cutting average setup time 30% with a documented checklist.'
- 'Maintained 98% SLA attainment on a queue of 200+ supported endpoints managed via Intune and an RMM platform.'
- 'Authored 10 knowledge-base articles that reduced repeat tickets for common Outlook and VPN issues.'

## How the ATS Sees You

An ATS reads your file as text and matches it against the posting. Two things matter: **format** (so it parses) and **keywords** (so it ranks).

**Format for parsing.** Keep it boringly standard: a single-column layout, common headings (*Summary, Skills, Experience, Certifications, Education*), a normal font, and a **text-based PDF or .docx**, never a scanned image. Avoid putting critical details inside images, text boxes, headers/footers, or multi-column tables, parsers often drop them. Simplicity here is not unsophisticated; it is correct.

**Keywords for ranking.** The posting tells you which words matter. If a desktop-support JD repeatedly says *Active Directory, Intune, ticketing, imaging, Office 365, troubleshooting*, those exact truthful terms belong on your resume because you have practiced them. Put them where they read naturally, in a skills section and woven into bullets, not crammed in white text or stuffed at the bottom. Hidden-text tricks are easy to detect and end your candidacy.

## Mirror the Job Description, Honestly

For each application, do a quick keyword pass: read the posting, list its recurring tools and phrases, and make sure every term you genuinely know appears in your resume using the posting's wording. If a JD says 'Microsoft Entra ID' and you wrote 'Azure AD', update it to match. This is **alignment, not invention**. Only mirror skills you can defend in an interview, because every term on the page is fair game for questioning. A gap you spot, a tool you have never touched, is something to go learn in your lab, not something to fake.

## The Skills Section

For helpdesk and desktop support, group skills so a reviewer and a parser both find them:

- **Operating systems:** Windows 10/11, basic macOS and Linux support.
- **Identity and directory:** Active Directory, Microsoft Entra ID, group policy, password and account management.
- **Endpoint management:** Microsoft Intune, Windows Autopilot, imaging, RMM (for example NinjaOne).
- **Productivity and cloud:** Microsoft 365 admin, Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive.
- **Support practice:** ticketing systems (ServiceNow, Jira, Zendesk), ITIL fundamentals, SLAs, remote support.
- **Scripting:** basic PowerShell.

List what is true for you. Pin the terms that match your target postings near the front.

## Map Your Certifications to the Role

Certifications tell a story; present them as a ladder mapped to the work:

- **CompTIA A+ (220-1201 & 220-1202)** - the entry-level foundation (hardware, OS, core troubleshooting). The baseline most helpdesk postings expect. Cite the current exam codes; the 220-1101/1102 series retired in September 2025.
- **CompTIA Network+ (N10-009)** - networking foundations (DNS, DHCP, subnets) that let you reason about connectivity issues.
- **Microsoft MD-102 (Endpoint Administrator Associate)** - deploying and securing Windows devices with Intune; maps directly to desktop-support and modern-management roles.
- **Microsoft MS-102 (Microsoft 365 Administrator)** - tenant administration: identity, licensing, Exchange Online, security and compliance. Note honestly that passing MS-102 earns the **Microsoft 365 Certified: Administrator Expert** certification only when you also hold a qualifying associate cert such as MD-102, so MS-102 plus MD-102 is the natural pairing for a Tier 2 / M365 path.

State status accurately: *certified*, *exam scheduled*, or *in progress*. That shows momentum without overclaiming. Put earned certs near the top of the resume, where they are an instant credibility signal for a career-changer.

## Put It Together: One Page, Tailored

Keep it to **one page** at this stage of your career. A workable order: a two-line summary naming your target role and edge (*Help Desk / Desktop Support technician, CompTIA A+ and Network+, hands-on with Active Directory, Microsoft 365, and Intune; portfolio linked*); your **portfolio link**; certifications; a skills section mirroring the JD; then experience and projects with quantified bullets, your capstone counts here. Tailor the summary, skills order, and top bullets to each posting. Save as a text-based PDF, name the file professionally (`firstname-lastname-helpdesk-resume.pdf`), and proofread twice, typos undercut a support candidate whose whole job is attention to detail.

## Avoid the Common Mistakes

A few errors sink otherwise strong helpdesk resumes, so check for them deliberately. Do not write a wall of unquantified duties, every bullet should carry a number or a concrete outcome. Do not use a dense two-column template full of graphics and icons; it looks modern but often confuses the parser and buries your keywords. Do not list every technology you have ever heard of, a focused, truthful skills section reads as more credible than an exhaustive one. Do not bury your certifications at the bottom; for a career-changer they are a headline asset. And do not send the same file to every employer, a thirty-second tailoring pass per posting dramatically raises your callback rate. Finally, keep one master resume holding all your bullets and metrics, then copy and trim it for each application; that way tailoring is fast and you never lose a strong line you might need for the next role.

## The Deliverable

By the end of this lesson you should have a one-page, ATS-friendly resume tailored to a specific real posting: clean format, quantified bullets drawn from your capstone and any real work, a skills section that mirrors the JD truthfully, and your certifications mapped to the role with honest status. Paired with the portfolio from the last lesson, it is a credible, verifiable case that you are ready for a first helpdesk or desktop-support job.
