## The First Filter

Your portfolio proves you can do the work, but it is rarely the first thing anyone reads. For most roles, the first filter is an **applicant tracking system** (ATS) that parses and ranks your resume on keyword relevance, followed by a recruiter who skims the survivors for roughly six seconds each. A resume that does not parse cleanly or does not contain the right terms never reaches a human, no matter how strong your projects are. This lesson makes your resume win both filters: machine-readable, keyword-aligned, and built around quantified outcomes a recruiter believes at a glance.

The AI PM twist is that the vocabulary is specific and current. Postings ask for experience with LLM features, evals, prompt design, RAG, roadmapping, experimentation, north-star metrics, and Responsible AI. Your resume has to speak that language truthfully, because you have done exactly that work in this course.

## The Bullet Formula

Replace task lists with outcomes. The reliable structure is **what you did + how you did it + the measurable impact**:

- Weak: *'Worked on an AI feature.'*
- Strong: *'Shipped an LLM-powered support assistant; designed the eval suite and human-in-the-loop fallback, cutting average handle time ~28% and deflecting 40% of tier-1 tickets.'*

Three habits make bullets land. **Start with an ownership verb** (led, shipped, defined, drove) rather than 'responsible for' or 'helped.' **Name the how** with real PM mechanisms (PRD, golden-set eval, A/B test, metric tree, guardrails) so the bullet demonstrates method, not just outcome. **Quantify**, and where the headline metric could hide a regression, pair it with a guardrail: '40% deflection while holding hallucination rate under a pre-set 2% threshold.' Numbers make claims credible, and a guardrail metric signals senior judgment.

Even simulated capstone work quantifies, honestly. State a **projected or modeled** impact with the reasoning and signal that the project was self-directed. Presenting a projection as a realized production result, or inflating it, collapses the moment an interviewer probes.

A quick way to upgrade a bullet is to ask three questions of it: *Did I lead or assist?*, *What method did I use?*, and *What changed because of it?* If a bullet cannot answer the third question, it is describing activity, not impact, and either needs a number or does not belong on a one-page resume. Cut filler verbs like 'assisted with,' 'helped on,' and 'involved in,' which quietly demote you to a bystander in your own work. Strong bullets also avoid stacking adjectives ('successfully delivered a robust, cutting-edge solution'); the metric carries the persuasion, so let it.

## Mirror the Job Description

Both ATS ranking and recruiter keyword searches reward relevance to the specific role. For each application, align your resume's language to the posting's **real terms**, but only for skills you genuinely have and can defend. If a JD emphasizes 'evaluation frameworks,' 'prompt design,' 'experimentation,' or 'stakeholder alignment,' and you did those, use those exact phrases. Read several postings for your target title and note the recurring vocabulary; that intersection is your keyword set.

This is alignment, not keyword-stuffing. Do not paste a hidden block of terms in white text, modern parsers and recruiters catch it, and do not claim a tool you have never touched. A gap in the JD is something to learn, not a word to fake.

Watch for two practical traps. First, **acronyms and their expansions** are not interchangeable to a parser: if a posting says 'large language models (LLMs)', include both the spelled-out term and the acronym at least once so you match either search. Second, **title language matters**, a profile and resume that say 'Product Manager' will not surface for a recruiter searching 'AI Product Manager' or 'Technical Product Manager,' so where it is truthful, use the specific title language the roles you want actually use. The goal is to be findable for the exact roles you can do, not to game irrelevant searches.

## The Skills Section

A scannable skills section helps both the ATS and the recruiter, and it should map truthfully to what you actually did:

- **AI/ML product:** LLM features, evals and golden sets, prompt design, RAG awareness, hallucination and safety guardrails, human-in-the-loop design.
- **Product craft:** PRDs, roadmapping, RICE and prioritization, opportunity assessment, north-star metrics and metric trees.
- **Data & experimentation:** A/B testing, analytics (Amplitude, Mixpanel), SQL basics if you have them.
- **Responsible AI:** Responsible AI practices, awareness of the EU AI Act and its risk-based obligations.

List tools you can speak to, not aspirational ones. Each line here is a question an interviewer may open.

## Format for the Machine and the Human

A resume must survive a parser and a six-second skim:

- **One page** for most candidates; two only with substantial relevant experience.
- **Single-column layout.** Avoid multi-column designs, tables, and text boxes, many parsers scramble them.
- **Real text, not graphics.** Never put skills or contact details inside an image, and keep key content out of headers and footers, which some parsers ignore.
- **Standard headings** (Experience, Skills, Education, Projects) and a common font. Save as PDF unless the posting requests .docx.
- **Front-load impact.** A short summary line and your strongest, most relevant bullets go up top where the six-second skim lands.

The summary line deserves special care because it is the first thing the recruiter reads. Make it a positioning sentence, not an objective: 'AI Product Manager who works problem-first, specifies LLM behavior and guardrails, and proves impact with evals and metrics' does more than three lines of 'seeking a challenging role.' For career-changers, lead the experience section with the most relevant work, your capstone and AI projects, even if that means listing a self-directed project block above an older unrelated job. The reader's eye should land on AI product evidence within the first third of the page, because that is the window the six-second skim actually covers.

## Certifications: Honest Supplements

Product management is experience- and portfolio-led; **no single credential is required** to be hired, and you should never imply otherwise. Position certifications as supplements that show commitment and a shared vocabulary, sitting behind your demonstrated outcomes:

- **AIPMM Certified Product Manager (CPM)** — a product-management credential from the AIPMM association; signals you know standard PM frameworks.
- **Pragmatic Institute** — well-known product management and product marketing training and certifications, strong on market-driven frameworks.
- **Google Project Management Professional Certificate** — a broad, accessible foundation in planning, stakeholders, and execution. Note it is a *project* management certificate; Google does not offer a dedicated product-management one, so describe it accurately rather than implying a product credential.

List certifications in a dedicated line near education, never above your projects or impact. In an interview, a credential gets a sentence; your portfolio gets the conversation. If you are choosing whether to invest, treat any of these as a modest signal-booster, not the thing that lands the offer, the case study from Lesson 1 does that.

## Tailor and Ship

Keep one master resume with every strong bullet, then **tailor per application**: reorder to surface the most relevant experience, swap in the posting's real keywords, and trim to one page. A quick keyword-match check against the JD (manually or with a tool like Jobscan) catches obvious gaps. The deliverable of this lesson is a one-page, ATS-clean, metric-driven resume plus a repeatable tailoring habit, the document that gets your portfolio in front of a human.
